title: She told us she was a feminist.

poems:
  - text: |+
      She wore ripped jeans.

      I remember the first day she walked into class.
      As a closeted 20-year old,
      I dreamed in the margins of the Norton Anthology.
      She was the kind of woman
      who looked like she spent her life being called
      “too much.”

      She leaned against the desk —
      as young professors do,
      cool and careless
      bangs sweeping across her eyes,
      silver rings tapping the podium,
      her voice rising in chalk dust.

      I’ll tell you: not many English classes kept me that awake

      But I was young
      and trying to be someone.
      Full of Pride and Prejudice –
      That’s when I began to question,
      when rules could split at the seams,
      that language could bend and play.

      Every time she walked into that room
      I thought about the possibilities of living freely.
      – I do think she loved
      hearing the sound of her own voice.
      I sort of get that now.
      But, I was new to English Lit.
      To theory.
      To find desire in prose or iambic pentameter.

      I tried to participate, often saying the word “omniscience” wrong.
      But she let me mistake a little, with a smirk and a hand on her hips.

      I began to love the smell
      of old paperbacks.
      There were feminist buttons pinned to her bag,
      We started to read
      Jane Austen,
      Virginia Woolf,
      Adrienne Rich –
      I wrote and taped quotes
      all over my notebooks.

      There were photocopied pages
      folded into her books,
      like little exchanges of power.
      – Yes, I know,
      I watched her too much.
      The way queer girls do
      observing who they like –
      who they want to be like –
      before they actually learn
      how to read the room.

      But she wasn’t actually gay.
      Maybe she was just one of those women
      who knew how to free herself fully.
      Because queerness arrived first
      as possibility,
      as dreamlike,
      as playful coyness
      as language would tongue-tie
      itself into promiscuity.
      So much room for interpretation.
      How to read a room with this woman
      as a woman who was told she took up too much space.

      I thought about all the women before us —
      women reading in locked bedrooms,
      women talking over shared cigarettes
      women kissing in parking lots
      after bars closed,
      women folding love letters
      into coat pockets,
      women teaching other women how not to disappear
      the kind who carry
      books under one arm
      a coffee cup with a lipstick stain in the other.

      “Then came the most exquisite moment of her whole life,
      passing a stone urn with flowers in it.
      Sally stopped; picked a flower; kissed her on the lips.”
      I read those lines alone in my room.
      That’s when I started smoking.
      And suddenly my body
      made terrible, beautiful sense to me.
      Virginia Woolf reached out of the page
      and split me open.

      After that,
      I noticed women everywhere.
      On trains.
      In bookstores.
      Outside bars after midnight
      with Doc Martens scraping pavement.
      Women laughing too loudly.
      Women refusing to be told what to do.
      Women who looked like
      they knew permission
      was a ridiculous idea invented by men.
      So somewhere between lit class
      and cigarettes,
      and alcohol
      I bloomed . . .
      between creating my own “room of one’s own”
      between Sappho and Woolf,
      between the confusion of not knowing
      if I wanted her
      or wanted to be like her –

      But as time went on
      I learned that
      the patriarchy doesn’t always arrive
      wearing a man’s face.
      I saw her
      in department gatherings
      In hallways
      In the small economies of power
      That I started to learn what universities run on.
      To see her and
      learn her place in the story of the university.
      She holds onto her seat
      while clawing through
      other women who are still fighting to get inside.

      And suddenly the ripped jeans looked different.
      A feminist mistaken,
      it was not rebellion,
      Performance.
      Branding.
      Not resistance.
      You can’t call yourself a feminist
      if doing so
      requires another woman to shrink.
      Just a silhouette who banked on privilege
      and whiteness –
      Because now I know . . .
      the less impressive her vocabulary actually is.

      Today, when I stand in front of the classroom
      I remember how foolishly
      I idolized her
      the way young women do
      when they are starving for examples.
      But you can’t say
      you are here
      to break glass ceilings
      if you plan to keep
      all the power for yourself.
      Because the truth is,
      the real truth, is
      that language is easy –
      Especially when you find your own.

authorBio: >+
  Danielle Bacibianco, PhD (she/her), is a queer writer, playwright, and
  researcher whose work explores the intersections of addiction, recovery,
  identity, and emotional survival. Her writing is marked by visceral honesty
  and lyrical intensity, often tracing the gray space between destruction and
  transformation. Drawing from lived experience, Danielle examines themes of
  longing, self-reclamation, and the complexities of healing from alcohol and
  drug addiction. 


  Danielle's original 60-minute play, The Plans We Made, was invited to take the stage at the Midtown International Theatre Festival (New York City, June 2026). Her play first took the stage as a 10-minute original at the Women’s Playwright Collective New Voices Festival (Staten Island, New York, 2025). Aside, her scholarly work has appeared in the following academic journals: College English, Reflections: A Journal of Community-Engaged Writing and Rhetoric, and Writers: Craft and Context.
